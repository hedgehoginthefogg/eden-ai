      Quiz yourself on the tips

      You’ll get a question about a card. Write your answer from memory, rate how sure you are, then turn the card over and compare your answer with the model answer.

      Who’s playing?

      You take turns to answer the same question without seeing each other’s answer, then turn the card over together and compare. You each score 2 points for an answer you got and 1 for an answer you got part of. Two-player games aren’t saved to your quiz history.

      You each use your own phone or computer, sitting together or on a call. One of you answers a question while the other checks the answer, then you swap. To start, one of you chooses the cards below and gets a code. The other enters that code here.

      A random mix

      Cards from every category, mixed together. Cards you haven’t tried, or got wrong last time, come up more often.

      One category

      Every card in one category, in a mixed order.

      Cards you choose

      Pick the cards yourself on the table, then start the quiz.

      Why the quiz works like this

      You write the answer from memory

      Each card has its own question about its main idea. Pulling an idea out of memory strengthens it more than reading it again. Writing the answer in your own words makes you do that retrieving yourself, rather than recognising the right option in a list (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). Even a partial answer counts.

      You rate your confidence before you look

      Reading something makes it feel familiar, and familiarity is easy to mistake for knowing it (Koriat and Bjork, 2005). Rating how sure you are first, then comparing, shows where your confidence runs ahead of what you can recall, so you know which cards to spend more time on.

      You mark your own answer

      Comparing your words with the card makes you notice exactly what you missed. That comparison is the feedback, and it sticks better because you did the work of spotting the gap.

      The random mix shuffles categories together

      Mixing topics feels harder than taking them one at a time, but it helps you tell similar ideas apart and remember them for longer (Kornell and Bjork, 2008). Difficulties like this, which slow you down now but help later, are what Bjork and Bjork (2011) call desirable difficulties.

      Cards you missed come back

      The random mix favours cards you haven’t tried or got wrong, especially ones you last saw more than a week ago. Recalling something again after a gap of days strengthens memory more than repeating it straight away (Cepeda and colleagues, 2006).

      Hints are there if you need them

      Try without the picture first. If you’re stuck, the hint gives you a nudge rather than the answer, so you still do some of the recalling.

      Playing with someone else

      In two-player mode you both answer before either of you sees the card. Comparing your answers, and talking through where they differ, makes you both explain your thinking. Explaining an idea in your own words helps you understand it and spot the gaps in it (Chi and colleagues, 1994).

      Playing on two devices

      One of you answers while the other checks the answer against its key points, then you swap. Checking someone else’s answer against a model answer builds evaluative judgement, the ability to tell what makes an answer good (Tai and colleagues, 2018). Explaining what was missing helps you both remember it.

      Start small

      Five cards is a good first quiz. Getting some right early on makes it easier to keep going and take on more.

            Add the tips to your home screen

            You can keep these tips on your phone or tablet like an app, and open them without the browser’s address bar. Once you’ve opened them, they also work without an internet connection.

            iPhone and iPad

            1. Open this page in Safari.
            2. Tap the Share button, a square with an arrow pointing up. On some versions of iOS it’s inside the More (•••) menu.
            3. Tap Add to Home Screen, then Add.

            Android

            1. Open this page in Chrome.
            2. Tap the menu (three dots) at the top right.
            3. Tap Add to home screen or Install app, then follow the prompts.
